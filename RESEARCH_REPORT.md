# Honest daily models predict risk, not direction

The evidence says that next-day direction for SPY, QQQ and BTC-USD is only barely predictable. Careful out-of-sample studies of index-level or small-universe daily direction land at roughly **51–56% accuracy (AUC about 0.51–0.55)**. In the most careful small-universe test, plain regularized logistic regression beat random forests, which overfit. The best-documented "edge" in this whole literature is not a direction call at all. It is **volatility targeting**: holding less when recent volatility is high. That robustly raises equity Sharpe ratios by about 0.1. The famous ML papers reporting 0.45% a day or Sharpe ratios above 5 solve a different, easier problem: ranking hundreds of stocks or coins against each other. Even those edges went negative after costs after about 2010, and published anomalies lose about 58% of their returns after publication. For the quant-lab project this changes priorities. The planned stack (logistic regression, random forest, LightGBM, expanding walk-forward, costs, a random-strategy test) is a sound core. What it lacks: calibrated probabilities judged against a base-rate baseline, a volatility-targeted buy-and-hold benchmark, a directional-skill test that accounts for the base rate, a log of every configuration tried so the final Sharpe can be deflated, and a hold-out long enough to mean something. A 12-month hold-out has a Sharpe standard error of about 1.0, so it cannot tell a good strategy from a lucky one. The most valuable additions are a few well-evidenced features (short-term reversal, volatility regime, the VIX term structure, slow trend) and a default-long "step aside when risk is high" design, rather than daily flipping between long and flat.

## Index timing is a far harder problem than the headline papers solve

Start with the academic baseline, because it sets expectations for everything else. Welch and Goyal tested the classic equity-premium predictors (dividend yield, earnings yield, interest rates, term and default spreads) and found that out of sample none of them beats simply forecasting the historical average return. Their conclusion was that "for all practical purposes, the equity premium has not been predictable" ([Welch & Goyal 2008](https://nber.org/papers/w10483)). Two later papers recovered small but real gains with simple discipline. Campbell and Thompson imposed common-sense restrictions, such as truncating negative premium forecasts at zero, and found out-of-sample explanatory power that is "small" but "economically meaningful" ([Campbell & Thompson 2008](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:21:y:2008:i:4:p:1509-1531)). Rapach, Strauss and Zhou showed that **averaging many weak forecasts** beats the historical mean consistently, because averaging reduces model instability ([Rapach, Strauss & Zhou 2010](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/354918)). These are monthly results. The two lessons that carry over to a daily model are to *shrink probabilities toward the base rate* and to *average models rather than pick the backtest winner*.

Theory also says daily direction is close to the hardest horizon. Christoffersen and Diebold show that volatility dynamics create predictable *sign* behaviour whenever the expected return is non-zero, even when the expected return itself is not predictable. That is the theoretical reason volatility belongs in a direction classifier. They also argue that this sign dependence is weakest at very high frequencies like daily and very low frequencies like annual, and strongest at intermediate horizons ([Christoffersen & Diebold 2006](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10009)). In plain terms, a model asked to predict tomorrow is working where the signal is faintest. Predicting the direction over the next five days may be somewhat easier.

The machine-learning headlines mostly come from **cross-sectional stock selection**, not index timing. Krauss, Do and Huck trained deep nets, gradient-boosted trees and random forests on S&P 500 *constituents*. They went long the 10 stocks most likely to beat the median and short the 10 least likely, and earned more than 0.45% a day before costs, 1992–2015 ([Krauss, Do & Huck 2017](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:ejores:v:259:y:2017:i:2:p:689-702)). Fischer and Krauss reported **about 54.3% directional accuracy** and a Sharpe of 5.8 before costs for an LSTM on the same universe ([Fischer & Krauss 2018](https://iwf.rw.fau.de/files/2015/12/11-2017.pdf)). Ranking stocks against each other cancels out the market's own move and harvests reversal across hundreds of names, so the noise diversifies away. A single-asset timing model gets none of that diversification. At the same hit rate it earns a far lower Sharpe.

Direct evidence on index direction is thin and sobering. Becker and Leschinski studied the 30 Dow stocks out of sample over 2004–2017. The best model was **logistic regression at 51.99%**, and **random forest was worst at 50.51%**, against naive benchmarks of 50.03–50.86%. They concluded that flexible ML overfits ([Macrosynergy summary of Becker & Leschinski](https://macrosynergy.com/?p=40742)). A 2024 study found that for LSTM, GBT and random-forest index-direction classifiers, accuracy "stays close to chance". The profits it did report came from controlling exposure, not from accurate direction calls ([EmergentMind summary of Finnegan et al. 2024](https://www.emergentmind.com/open-problems/predict-daily-market-direction-remains-unsolved)). The most credible positive index result is monthly, not daily. Gu, Kelly and Xiu built S&P 500 forecasts bottom-up from roughly 30,000 individual stock predictions and raised the out-of-sample timing Sharpe **from 0.51 to 0.77** ([Gu, Kelly & Xiu 2020](https://www.aqr.com:443/-/media/AQR/Documents/Journal-Articles/Empirical-Asset-Pricing-in-Machine-Learning.pdf?sc_lang=en)). That needs stock-level data that quant-lab does not have. For a counterpoint, Kelly, Malamud and Zhou prove that heavily parameterized models can extract more market-return predictability than simple ones ([Kelly, Malamud & Zhou 2024](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:79:y:2024:i:1:p:459-503)). That is a real argument for complexity, but it rests on monthly data and large feature sets, and it does not make daily index timing easy.

Bitcoin looks more predictable on paper and collapses after costs. Jaquart, Dann and Weinhardt predicted BTC over 1- to 60-minute horizons. At 60 minutes, logistic regression scored 53.9% against 55.7–56.0% for gradient boosting and LSTM. Strategies that made up to 39% a month before costs turned **negative at 30 bp round-trip costs** ([Jaquart, Dann & Weinhardt 2021](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000150665/149388567)). A study of 14,919 technical rules on crypto found many that survived multiple-testing corrections in sample, yet **the best in-sample rules lost money on Bitcoin** in the 2018 out-of-sample half-year ([Annals of Operations Research](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10479-019-03357-1)). A 2026 synthesis concludes that daily BTC predictability "appears real" but "may not survive transaction costs". It recommends walk-forward tests, multi-regime hold-outs, naive baselines and Diebold-Mariano tests ([Baquero 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00071)).

Decay is the final reason for humility. Krauss et al.'s ensemble earned an after-cost Sharpe of 6.7 in 1992–2001, but **every model had negative after-cost returns (−14% to −25% a year) in 2010–2015**. The authors blame wider ML adoption ([Krauss et al. working paper](https://www.iwf.rw.fau.de/files/2016/03/03-2016.pdf)). Across 97 published predictors, returns fall 26% out of sample and **58% after publication** ([McLean & Pontiff 2016](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/359722)). Technical analysis looked profitable mostly "until the early 1990s" ([Park & Irwin 2007](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jecsur/v21y2007i4p786-826.html)). Sullivan, Timmermann and White tested 7,846 technical rules. The best one looked spectacular on the Dow from 1897 to 1986, but out of sample in 1987–1996 its data-snooping-adjusted p-value was 0.341, and on S&P futures it was 0.908. That is no edge at all ([Sullivan, Timmermann & White 1999](https://bashtage.github.io/kevinsheppard.com/files/teaching/mfe/advanced-econometrics/Sullivan_Timmermann_White.pdf)). The honest planning assumption for quant-lab is an AUC of about 0.51–0.53 on the hold-out. If a model shows much more, suspect a bug before believing a discovery.

## Three daily signal families survive scrutiny: reversal, volatility, slow trend

Every feature below must be computable from data known at the close of day *t*, and the target is the return from close *t* to close *t+1*. The basic building block is the log return `r_t = ln(C_t / C_{t−1})`, the percentage change in the closing price expressed on a log scale so that daily returns add up across days.

### Short-term reversal is the best-supported daily equity feature

Since about 2000, daily index returns have tended to *reverse*: a run of up days is slightly more likely to be followed by a down day. Baltussen, van Bekkum and Da studied 20 indices in 15 countries. Before 1999, average next-day autocorrelation was about +0.05 and positive for 16 of 20 indices. After March 1999 it was negative for 16 of 20, and a five-day version (MAC(5)) was **negative for all 20**. They link this to the growth of indexing and ETF arbitrage. A strategy trading against it earned a gross Sharpe of **0.67 on the S&P 500**, but the authors warn it "perhaps is not exploitable to many investors after accounting for transaction costs" ([Baltussen, van Bekkum & Da 2019](https://personal.eur.nl/vanbekkum/2018%20JFE%20BaltussenVanBekkumDa.pdf)). Their sample ends in 2016, and the research notes could not verify the sign after that, so the project's walk-forward should check whether the effect survives. The simple version to compute is the sum of the last five daily returns, `R5_t = r_t + r_{t−1} + … + r_{t−4}`. The model should learn a *negative* weight on it if reversal is still present. The spec's lagged returns already contain this information. Adding `R5` as one explicit feature makes it easy for the logistic regression to use and easy for a beginner to interpret.

A related decomposition splits each day into an overnight gap and an intraday move: `g_t = ln(O_t / C_{t−1})` (the jump from yesterday's close to today's open) and `i_t = ln(C_t / O_t)` (the move during trading), so that `r_t = g_t + i_t`. The overnight/intraday literature is genuinely mixed. One study using 24-hour E-mini futures found that **the four hours around the European open account for the entire average market return** ([Bondarenko & Muravyev 2023](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/market-return-around-the-clock-a-puzzle/089E33AC0B4D3B9A02CBA31EDF6505B3)). Another found about 40% of the aggregate US premium earned intraday in 1993–2013, with its strongest result being cross-sectional ([Lou, Polk & Skouras 2019](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/polk/research/TugOfWar.pdf)). For a close-to-close target, `g_t` and `i_t` are cheap, free features from data yfinance already supplies, but they carry a low prior.

### Volatility predicts risk and regime far better than direction

Volatility features matter mostly because they predict *future volatility*, and through Christoffersen and Diebold's mechanism they feed weakly into direction. The spec's close-to-close rolling volatility is `RV_n = (252/n)·Σ r²` over the last *n* days, the annualized average squared return. The daily high and low carry extra information. The Yang-Zhang estimator combines the overnight gap, the open-to-close move and the intraday high-low range into a less noisy volatility estimate from the same OHLC bars ([LuxAlgo, Yang-Zhang estimator](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/yang-zhang-estimator/)). It is a drop-in improvement for any feature that uses rolling volatility.

The VIX family carries the most information. The VIX measures expected 30-day volatility implied by option prices. In minute-level ML work on S&P returns, **VIX was the single strongest predictor** ([Ferreira & Medeiros 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.15108)). Two free derived signals are worth adding. The first is the **variance risk premium (VRP)**: the gap between implied and realized variance, `VRP_t = (VIX_t/100)² − RV_21,t`. When options price in much more volatility than has recently occurred, investors are paying for crash insurance, and that has predicted higher equity returns over the following one to three months, with monthly R² of roughly 1.5–4% ([Drechsler & Yaron 2011](https://core.ac.uk/download/132270718.pdf)). The second is the **VIX term structure**, `TS1_t = VIX_t / VIX3M_t`. A value above 1 ("backwardation") means near-term fear exceeds longer-term fear. Backwardation in the VIX futures curve has been a significant *contrarian* signal for subsequent S&P 500 returns, while normal contango carries no signal. That rests on a single 2010–2017 sample without an out-of-sample test ([Fassas & Hourvouliades 2019](https://mdpi.com/1911-8074/12/3/113)). Most published VIX term-structure research predicts returns of *volatility products*, not of the index itself ([Johnson 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/risk-premia-and-the-vix-term-structure/56572D1F060448571BD8F597C732D9C3)). Both signals are slow-moving conditioning variables, not daily alpha. Note that `^VIX3M` history begins in 2007, which matters for a dataset starting in 1999.

### Trend works at monthly horizons, so use it as a regime switch

Time-series momentum, the sign of the past 1- to 12-month return, is one of the most robust findings in finance. It has been positive in every decade since 1880 across 67 markets ([Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen 2017](https://fairmodel.econ.yale.edu/ec439/hurst.pdf)). For equity index futures, the 12-month lookback with a one-month hold has a t-statistic of 3.77 ([Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen 2012](https://fairmodel.econ.yale.edu/ec439/jpde.pdf)). Its home is roughly a **one-month holding period**, though, and the strong daily moving-average results on the Dow failed out of sample once data snooping was corrected ([Sullivan, Timmermann & White 1999](https://bashtage.github.io/kevinsheppard.com/files/teaching/mfe/advanced-econometrics/Sullivan_Timmermann_White.pdf)). The spec's 200-day moving-average baseline and MA-distance features are therefore well chosen as *regime* inputs. Adding `ln(C_t/C_{t−k})` for k = 63, 126 and 252 trading days captures the documented momentum horizons. Lookbacks beyond 12 months partially reverse and should be avoided.

### Bitcoin's documented predictors run over days to weeks

Crypto shows a "strong time-series momentum effect", and investor attention (Google searches, Twitter) "strongly forecasts" returns ([Liu & Tsyvinski 2021](https://nber.org/papers/w24877)). Cross-sectional crypto momentum is significant at **1- to 4-week** horizons and fades by 8 weeks ([Liu, Tsyvinski & Wu 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25882.pdf)). Holding BTC only when its price is above a 5- to 100-day moving average produced gross Sharpe ratios of 2.2–2.6 versus 2.0 for buy-and-hold, and cut maximum drawdown from −89.5% to about −66% to −70%, over 2010–2017 ([CXO summary of Detzel et al.](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/currency-trading/timing-bitcoin-with-smas/)). In-house research found BTC trading at a new 10- to 50-day high showed strong follow-through ([Quantpedia 2022](https://quantpedia.com/trend-following-and-mean-reversion-in-bitcoin/)). All of these come from an early, illiquid market and should be expected to have decayed since spot ETFs launched in 2024. Calendar effects in BTC show "no consistent or persistent patterns" ([Quantpedia summary of Baur et al.](https://quantpedia.com/are-there-any-simple-calendar-effects-in-bitcoin-market/)). The daily BTC–S&P correlation jumped from 0.01 in 2017–19 to 0.36 in 2020–21 ([IMF](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USIMF/bulletins/30520df)). That is co-movement on the same day, and no source in the notes shows that lagged equity moves predict BTC's next day. The spec's as-of cross-asset features are fine to keep, with weekend rows flagged as stale.

### Most other popular features are decayed or redundant

RSI, MACD and Bollinger bands are re-parameterizations of recent returns and moving-average distance. The spec's 20-day z-score, `(C_t − MA20)/SD20`, is nearly the same quantity as `C_t/MA20 − 1` divided by volatility. Highly correlated substitutes like these make feature importance unstable and add selection risk without new information. Calendar effects have mostly decayed for large-cap US indices. The pre-holiday premium is **not significant for the S&P 500 over 1983–2019 (t = 0.64)** ([Ko & Yang](https://cfr.ivo-welch.org/forthcoming/papers/ko2021pre.pdf)), and the classic turn-of-month window has "largely disappeared" for SPY ([QuantSeeker](https://www.quantseeker.com/p/turn-of-the-month-strategies-do-they)). The most economically grounded calendar effect is the **pre-FOMC drift**, about 50 bp in the 24 hours before scheduled Fed announcements over 1994–2011. Over 2011–2018 it survived only at meetings with a press conference ([NY Fed, Liberty Street Economics](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2018/11/the-pre-fomc-announcement-drift-more-recent-evidence.html)). It occurs only about eight days a year, so it is a cheap, low-prior dummy built from hard-coded scheduled dates. Macro variables such as yields, the dollar, credit spreads and gold fail even at monthly horizons ([Welch & Goyal 2008](https://nber.org/papers/w10483)). The notes found no reliable daily evidence for them, so they are not worth the extra downloads at this stage.

| Feature family | Evidence strength at daily horizon | Assets | Role in quant-lab |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short-term reversal (lags, `R5`) | Moderate, gross Sharpe ~0.67 post-1999, cost-fragile | SPY, QQQ | Keep and make explicit |
| Realized vol, Yang-Zhang vol | Strong for *vol*, weak for sign | All | Keep; use for sizing |
| VIX level/change, VIX/VIX3M, VRP | Moderate, mostly monthly | SPY, QQQ (and BTC as cross-asset) | Add |
| Trend / TSMOM 3–12 months, MA distance | Strong at monthly, weak daily | All | Keep as regime |
| BTC 1–4 week momentum, new-high flag | Moderate, early sample, likely decayed | BTC | Add |
| RSI, MACD, Bollinger, z-score | Redundant re-parameterizations | All | Keep at most one |
| Day of week, pre-holiday, turn-of-month | Decayed | All | Day-of-week low prior; drop pre-holiday |
| Pre-FOMC dummy | Economically motivated, small sample | SPY, QQQ | Optional |
| Yields, DXY, credit, gold | No daily evidence | All | Don't add |

## Simple, regularized, calibrated models beat flexible ones on this problem

The core model is **logistic regression**, which turns a weighted sum of features into a probability:

```
P(up_{t+1} | x_t) = 1 / (1 + exp(−(β₀ + β₁x₁ + … + β_kx_k)))
```

In plain language, each feature gets a weight. The weighted total is squashed through an S-shaped curve so it always lands between 0 and 1. Positive weights push the probability of an up day above the baseline. L2 regularization adds a penalty for large weights and keeps the model from chasing noise. That is the scikit-learn `C` parameter: smaller `C` means more shrinkage toward the base rate, which is the Campbell-Thompson lesson in code. This is the model that won Becker and Leschinski's test ([Macrosynergy](https://macrosynergy.com/?p=40742)), and in Fischer and Krauss's study it was competitive before costs but not significantly profitable after them ([Fischer & Krauss working paper](https://iwf.rw.fau.de/files/2015/12/11-2017.pdf)). It should be the model that everything else has to beat.

Random forests and gradient boosting (LightGBM) earn their place because they can find interactions, for example "reversal works only when VIX is high". Random forest was the best *single* model in Krauss et al. (after-cost Sharpe 1.90). An **equal-weight ensemble** of diverse models had the highest directional accuracy at every portfolio size ([Krauss et al. working paper](https://www.iwf.rw.fau.de/files/2016/03/03-2016.pdf)). This mirrors Rapach et al.'s finding that combinations are robust. Deep learning (LSTM, GRU) beat tree models by at most about 3 bp a day or 1 percentage point of accuracy, often not significantly ([Fischer & Krauss working paper](https://iwf.rw.fau.de/files/2015/12/11-2017.pdf); [Jaquart et al. 2021](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000150665/149388567)). The spec's exclusion of PyTorch and TensorFlow is supported by the evidence, not just by disk space. With only about 6,000 daily rows for SPY and fewer for BTC, tree models must be small and heavily regularized. The research notes suggest LightGBM with at most 15 leaves and at least 100 samples per leaf.

Two other model families are useful as *inputs* rather than as direction classifiers. **HAR-RV** forecasts tomorrow's volatility as a linear regression on average realized volatility over the past day, week and month ([Corsi 2009](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jfinec:v:7:y:2009:i:2:p:174-196)):

```
RV_{t+1} = b₀ + b_d·RV_t + b_w·RV_{t−4..t} + b_m·RV_{t−21..t} + ε
```

The idea is that volatility has a long memory. Yesterday matters, last week matters somewhat less, and last month a little. The model is a statsmodels OLS fit in three lines, and its forecast is exactly what a volatility-targeting sizer needs. **Hidden Markov / regime-switching models** ([Hamilton 1989](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ecm:emetrp:v:57:y:1989:i:2:p:357-84)) identify interpretable bull, correction and high-volatility bear states in both the S&P 500 and BTC ([Suda & Spiteri 2019](https://www.um.edu.mt/library/oar/bitstream/123456789/98539/1/Analysis%20and%20comparison%20of%20Bitcoin%20and%20S%20and%20P%20500%20market%20features%20using%20HMMs%20and%20HSMMs.pdf)). That study's headline returns used no costs and picked the best strategy after the fact. HMMs are also a leakage trap: the commonly used *smoothed* state path uses future data, and only *filtered* probabilities fitted on past data are safe. They are a later-stage experiment, not a priority.

### Calibration turns scores into probabilities a dashboard can show

A dashboard that says "54% chance of up" makes a promise. Of all the days it says 54%, about 54% should actually be up. Boosted trees distort their outputs, while bagged trees and logistic regression come out roughly calibrated. After correction, boosted trees and random forests give some of the best probabilities ([Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana 2005](https://mlanthology.org/icml/2005/niculescumizil2005icml-predicting)). **Platt (sigmoid) scaling** fits `p = 1/(1 + exp(A·f + B))` on the model's raw score *f*: a two-parameter logistic regression that bends the raw scores onto the observed frequencies. Isotonic regression is more flexible but "more prone to overfitting" below about 1,000 samples ([scikit-learn calibration guide](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html)). The scikit-learn guide does not cover time series, and its default `cv=5` shuffles across time. The calibrator must therefore be fit on a chronologically *later* slice of each training window than the one the model learned from.

Metrics need a baseline that already knows the market drifts up. Accuracy against 50% is misleading because stocks rise on slightly more than half of days, so "always up" scores above 50% with zero skill. The right reference is **climatology**: a constant forecast equal to the training window's up-day frequency. The scoring rules are:

```
Log loss = −mean[ y·ln(p) + (1−y)·ln(1−p) ]
Brier    =  mean[ (p − y)² ]
Brier skill score = 1 − Brier_model / Brier_climatology
```

Log loss punishes confident wrong calls very harshly. Brier is the average squared gap between forecast and outcome. The skill score shows what fraction of the climatology error the model removed. A Brier skill score of zero means the model knows nothing beyond the base rate, and with realistic daily edges a positive skill score of even 0.005 would be noteworthy. Finance papers almost never report calibration. The notes found no daily index-direction paper comparing Platt and isotonic or reporting Brier scores, so quant-lab is doing something the literature mostly skips. One more useful lens comes from crypto. Accuracy across all days was 52.9–54.1%, but rose to **57.5–59.5% on the 10% most-confident predictions** ([Jaquart, Köpke & Weinhardt 2022](https://doaj.org/article/cfc09801314441198e3e1c92a3914e54)). Reporting the hit rate by confidence bucket shows whether the probability ranking carries information even when the average edge is tiny.

## Volatility targeting is the best-evidenced edge, and it is about sizing

The single most robust improvement in this research does not predict direction at all. Moreira and Muir scale exposure by the inverse of last month's realized variance:

```
managed return_{t+1} = (c / σ̂²_t) · return_{t+1}
```

In words: when the past month was calm, hold more; when it was turbulent, hold less. The constant *c* just sets the average exposure. For the US market over 1926–2015 this produced an alpha of **4.86% a year** and lifted the Sharpe ratio from 0.42 to 0.51. It survives costs, with a break-even of about 56 bp ([Moreira & Muir 2017](https://amoreira2.github.io/alan-moreira.github.io/VolPortfolios_published.pdf)). The mechanism is that "changes in factor volatilities are not offset by proportional changes in expected returns" ([NBER w22208](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22208)). Harvey et al. confirmed it across 60 assets with a 10% volatility target. US equity Sharpe rose from **0.40 to 0.48–0.51**, insensitive to the volatility estimator. Tails, kurtosis and drawdowns shrank, but commodities did not improve ([Harvey et al. 2018](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1370354)). The steelman against it matters. Across 103 factor strategies, *real-time* volatility-managed versions generally had lower Sharpe ratios than unmanaged ones, because the in-sample scaling relationships were unstable ([Cederburg et al. 2020](https://alphaarchitect.com/does-portfolio-timing-based-on-volatility-signals-outperform-buy-and-hold/)). A 2024 multifactor reassessment also exists, but the notes did not read it ([DeMiguel et al. 2024](https://lbsresearch.london.edu/id/eprint/3716/1/The%20Journal%20of%20Finance%20-%202024%20-%20DeMIGUEL%20-%20A%20Multifactor%20Perspective%20on%20Volatility%E2%80%90Managed%20Portfolios.pdf)). Net of all this, volatility targeting for an equity index is well supported, and no peer-reviewed evidence specific to BTC was found. This is why a **volatility-targeted buy-and-hold** must be a baseline. It may capture most of the achievable improvement, and an ML model that cannot beat it adds nothing.

The **Kelly criterion** links a probability to a bet size. For a continuous asset the growth-maximizing weight is `f* = (μ − r)/σ²`: expected excess return divided by variance ([MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba 2010](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/157/Papers/Good_Bad_Kelly.pdf)). For an even-money bet with win probability *p* it simplifies to `f* = 2p − 1`, so a 53% probability means betting 6% of capital. The same paper is blunt about the danger. Kelly is "extremely sensitive to errors in estimated means". Betting twice Kelly gives **zero growth**, and half Kelly keeps 75% of the growth while raising the chance of doubling before halving from 0.67 to 0.89. The research notes ran the numbers for SPY with p = 0.53 and found that full Kelly implies roughly 4.8× leverage, which is absurd given estimation error. The practical rule is to treat Kelly as a ceiling, use a fraction of it (λ ≈ 0.25–0.5), and multiply by a volatility-targeting factor `σ_target/σ̂_t`. Then clip to [0, 1], because quant-lab is long-only with no leverage. A small no-trade band, such as rebalancing only when the target weight moves by more than 0.1, stops volatility scaling from generating daily churn.

Costs decide whether any of this survives. The spec's 0.05% per change for SPY and QQQ is *conservative*. SPY's average spread is about 0.0032% ([etf.com](https://www.etf.com/sections/news/etfs-highest-lowest-trading-spreads)), and the SEC Section 31 fee is $20.60 per million on sales since April 2026 ([SEC Fee Rate Advisory 2026-2](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/fee-rate-advisories/2026-2)), about 0.2 bp. The notes put realistic all-in costs at about 1–2 bp per side, so keeping 5 bp is honest and leaves room for slippage. BTC is the opposite. Coinbase Advanced's lowest tier charges **0.60% taker / 0.40% maker** ([Datawallet](https://www.datawallet.com/crypto/coinbase-fees)) and Kraken Pro's lowest tier 0.80% taker ([Datawallet](https://www.datawallet.com/crypto/kraken-fees-explained)). The spec's 0.15% is achievable only with maker orders at a mid-volume tier. A beginner's small account would pay four times that. As the notes calculate, a strategy flipping 100 times a year at 10 bp per side spends about 20% a year on costs, more than any plausible daily edge. The design response is to trade *less*: default long, step aside on strong risk signals, and smooth the position.

## Proving an edge is real takes more than one backtest

The spec's validation skeleton matches best practice. It uses expanding walk-forward, monthly refits, a one-day gap so the last training label never overlaps the first test day, and an untouched hold-out. For a one-day label, the leakage that López de Prado's *purging* targets is just that single overlapping row. *Embargo* (dropping rows after a test fold) matters only for k-fold or combinatorial purged CV, where training data sit after the test period. There, the notes suggest sizing it to the longest feature lookback or about 1% of the sample ([Wikipedia: Purged cross-validation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purged_cross-validation)). Combinatorial purged CV produces a distribution of Sharpe ratios instead of one path, and skfolio offers a ready-made splitter ([skfolio](https://skfolio.org/generated/skfolio.model_selection.CombinatorialPurgedCV.html)). For a beginner project, walk-forward is the right primary test. CPCV is an optional extra for model selection. For BTC, whose market structure changed sharply, a rolling window of about 3–4 years is worth running alongside the expanding window. The notes found no source giving an optimal length.

Four statistical tests answer different questions, and the project should use each for its own purpose. The **Pesaran-Timmermann test** asks whether up/down calls beat chance *given* how often the market and the model each say "up":

```
p̂*  = p̂_y·p̂_z + (1−p̂_y)(1−p̂_z)          expected hit rate if calls were random
S_n = (p̂ − p̂*) / √(v̂ − ŵ)  ~  N(0,1)
```

Here p̂ is the actual hit rate, p̂_y the share of up days and p̂_z the share of "up" predictions. A model that says "up" 90% of the time can show high accuracy with no skill, and this test catches that. It ships in statsmodels as `pesaran_timmermann` ([statsmodels](https://www.statsmodels.org/dev/generated/statsmodels.stats.diagnostic.pesaran_timmermann.html)). It cannot be computed for "always up", because that has no variation. The **Diebold-Mariano test** compares two forecasts' losses (say LightGBM against logistic regression on log loss) with a statistic of mean loss difference over its standard error. It is in statsmodels' development branch as `diebold_mariano_test`, so the installed version should be checked ([statsmodels](https://www.statsmodels.org/dev/generated/statsmodels.tsa.stattools.diebold_mariano_test.html)). **Clark-West** is the correct variant when the bigger model contains the simpler one, such as "features" versus "constant drift" ([Clark & West 2006](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/t0326/t0326.pdf)). **Hansen's SPA test** asks whether *any* of the models beats buy-and-hold after accounting for having tried several. It improves on White's Reality Check, whose p-value in Hansen's example jumped from 10.6% to 96.3% just from adding poor models ([Hansen 2005](https://scispace.com/pdf/a-test-for-superior-predictive-ability-1fbgz05jht.pdf)). It is a few lines with `arch.bootstrap.SPA` ([arch docs](https://arch.readthedocs.io/en/latest/multiple-comparison/generated/arch.bootstrap.SPA.html)).

The spec's 1,000-random-strategies check is a good teaching tool. It answers "is this timing better than random timing with the same exposure?", but it does not account for how many models, features and thresholds were tried before picking the reported one. That is the job of the **Deflated Sharpe Ratio**. First, the Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio gives the probability that the true Sharpe exceeds a benchmark, allowing for sample length and fat tails:

```
PSR(SR*) = Φ( (SR̂ − SR*)·√(n−1) / √(1 − γ₃·SR̂ + ((γ₄−1)/4)·SR̂²) )
```

Here SR̂ is the *daily* Sharpe, n the number of days, γ₃ skewness and γ₄ kurtosis ([Bernardini & de Castro 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.07217)). The DSR replaces SR* with the Sharpe you would expect from the *best of N useless strategies* ([Bailey & López de Prado 2014](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/deflated-sharpe.pdf)):

```
SR₀ = √Var(SR̂ₙ) · [ (1−γ_E)·Φ⁻¹(1 − 1/N) + γ_E·Φ⁻¹(1 − 1/(N·e)) ]
```

In plain terms, the more things you try, the higher a Sharpe you get by luck alone, and the DSR asks whether your winner clears *that* bar. The notes' worked example shows how quickly this bites. An annual Sharpe of 1.0 over five years has a PSR of about 0.99 against zero. After 100 trials whose Sharpes vary with a daily standard deviation of 0.02, the DSR falls to **about 0.67**, which is not significant (I re-ran this arithmetic). Computing it needs exactly one thing the spec does not yet do: a log of every configuration tried, with its out-of-sample daily returns. Harvey, Liu and Zhu's broader warning is that a new finding should clear **t > 3.0**, not 2.0, because "most claimed research findings in financial economics are likely false" ([Harvey, Liu & Zhu 2016](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592)). For a strategy's mean return, t is roughly the annual Sharpe times √years. Over the SPY sample that needs an annual Sharpe above about 0.6, and over BTC's 12 years above about 0.87.

The 12-month hold-out is the spec's weakest number, because one year of daily data has very little statistical power. By my arithmetic from the PSR denominator, the standard error of an annualized Sharpe ratio estimated from 252 days is about **1.0**. It is about 0.71 over two years and 0.45 over five. Directional accuracy over 252 days has a standard error of about 3.1 percentage points, so a "54%" hold-out result is consistent with anything from no skill to a large edge. The Minimum Track Record Length formula ([arXiv 2101.07217](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.07217)) says that confirming a true annual Sharpe of 0.5 against zero at 95% confidence takes about **11 years** of daily returns (my calculation). No hold-out can deliver that. The hold-out's job is therefore to *catch failure*, such as a collapse relative to walk-forward results, not to prove success. The notes recommend about two years, and the dashboard should show it with confidence intervals.

Feature importance needs the same care. LightGBM's built-in gain importance is in-sample and biased toward features that the model overfits ([scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/permutation_importance.html)). Correlated substitutes such as 5-, 10- and 20-day returns, RSI and z-score dilute each other. In one experiment, the weakest of six copies of a real signal ranked below eight pure-noise features ([MQL5 summary of AFML](https://www.mql5.com/en/articles/23564)). Out-of-sample permutation importance computed on *clusters* of correlated features is the robust alternative. Cluster-level rankings have proven far more stable across random seeds ([Chan 2021](https://epchan.blogspot.com/2021/01/the-amazing-efficacy-of-cluster-based-feature-selection.html?m=1)).

## Conclusion

The research reframes what quant-lab should expect to find. The probability of an up day for SPY, QQQ or BTC is almost always close to the base rate. The informative part of the forecast is *how far, and how reliably, it moves away from that rate*, and volatility forecasts of when to hold less are more reliable than direction forecasts. The most likely honest outcome of Stage 6 is that no direction model beats a volatility-targeted buy-and-hold after costs on the hold-out, with logistic regression roughly matching the tree models. Under the spec's own quality bar that is a PASS, and it reproduces what the literature found once costs, base rates and post-publication decay are taken seriously.

The second insight is about process. The cheapest, highest-value additions are bookkeeping, not modelling: a climatology baseline, a trial log, a base-rate-aware skill test and calibrated probabilities. Without them, any apparent edge cannot be told apart from the selection luck that sank most of the 7,846 rules Sullivan, Timmermann and White tested. With them, the project can say exactly how much evidence it has, which is the core skill of quant research. The best candidates for a real, durable effect worth testing first are short-term reversal in SPY/QQQ, the VIX term structure and variance risk premium as stress gauges, and a default-long, step-aside sizing design that trades rarely enough to survive costs.
